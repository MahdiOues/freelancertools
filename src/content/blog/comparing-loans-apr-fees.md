When you need a loan, the interest rate is the first number you see. But it is not the only number that matters. Two loans with the same interest rate can have dramatically different total costs depending on fees, terms, and how the interest is calculated. Comparing loans correctly saves you hundreds or thousands of dollars.

## Interest rate vs APR

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the principal, expressed as a percentage. The annual percentage rate (APR) includes the interest rate plus certain fees, giving you a more complete picture of the yearly cost.

APR was designed to make comparison shopping easier. If Loan A has a 6% interest rate with 2 points and Loan B has a 6.5% rate with no points, the APR tells you which is cheaper overall. But APR has limitations. It assumes you keep the loan for its full term, which many borrowers do not. If you plan to sell or refinance in a few years, the upfront costs matter more than the long-term APR.

## Common loan fees

Origination fees are charged by the lender for processing the loan. They typically range from 0.5% to 2% of the loan amount. On a $300,000 mortgage, a 1% origination fee is $3,000. Some lenders advertise no origination fees but offset them with higher interest rates.

Application fees cover the cost of processing your application. Some lenders waive them. Credit report fees are small — usually $30 to $50 — but they add up. Appraisal fees for mortgages can run $400 to $700. Underwriting fees cover the lender's review of your application.

Prepayment penalties are fees charged if you pay off the loan early. Avoid loans with prepayment penalties if possible. They limit your ability to refinance or sell the property.

## How to compare loans effectively

Look at the total cost of the loan, not just the monthly payment. Amonthly payment of $1,500 over 30 years costs $540,000 total. The same payment over 15 years costs $270,000 — half the total cost. Extending the term lowers your monthly payment but dramatically increases total interest.

Compare the same loan type and term. Comparing a 30-year fixed mortgage to a 5-year adjustable-rate mortgage is apples to oranges. Compare similar products with similar terms.

Request loan estimates from multiple lenders. In mortgage lending, the Loan Estimate form standardizes fees, making comparison straightforward. For personal loans, look at the total repayment amount — the sum of all payments.

## The trap of low monthly payments

Lenders often highlight low monthly payments. But low payments usually mean longer terms. A 72-month car loan has lower payments than a 36-month loan, but you pay interest for twice as long. The total interest on a $30,000 car loan at 7% over 72 months is about $6,700. Over 36 months, it is about $3,300.

Always calculate the total cost. Ask for the total repayment amount and compare that across lenders. A loan with slightly higher payments but a shorter term may be cheaper overall.

## When APR is misleading

APR assumes you keep the loan for its full term. For mortgages, most people sell or refinance within 5 to 10 years. If you pay a 1% origination fee on a 30-year loan but only keep it for 5 years, that fee is spread over 5 years in reality, not 30. Your effective APR is higher than stated.

For short-term loans like personal loans or auto loans, APR is more reliable because the terms are shorter and most borrowers pay them off as scheduled.
